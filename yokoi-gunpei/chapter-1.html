Chapter 1: The Age of Idea Toys (1966–1980)

~31 min read

Ultra Hand Ultra Hand

1966 (Showa 41). Price at launch: ¥600

Gunpei Yokoi joined Nintendo1 in 1965 (Showa 40). At the time, Nintendo's main products were hanafuda cards and playing cards — no one could have imagined the company would one day become "the Nintendo of the Famicom2." Yokoi himself felt the same way, and spoke of himself without hesitation: "I was a washout, plain and simple. Nintendo was the one that picked me up." In truth, after graduating from the Department of Electronics Engineering at Doshisha University, he failed the entrance exams at major electronics manufacturers one after another and could find no other employer willing to take him. Naturally, there was no way Nintendo would have work that made use of his knowledge of electronics engineering. Even when his classmates asked him, "What kind of work are you going to do at Nintendo?" Yokoi had no real answer.

His answer was utterly unremarkable. "Well, more than anything, I just didn't want to leave Kyoto. I didn't have any particular dreams about work — I figured if I could just coast along comfortably until retirement, that would be good enough." Or so he claimed.

The job he was given after joining the company was light work — equipment maintenance and inspections. If things had continued that way, Yokoi would have spent his career doing maintenance until retirement, and Nintendo would have remained nothing more than a hanafuda card manufacturer. However, because his work left him with far too much free time, Yokoi began whiling away his spare time making toys on the clock.

The person who noticed those toys was President Hiroshi Yamauchi3. From that point on, the fortunes of both Yokoi and Nintendo would take a dramatic turn. That toy was none other than the future "Ultra Hand."

What is particularly fascinating about the Ultra Hand is that President Yamauchi ordered him to "commercialize it as a game." Given that Nintendo at the time had just begun developing toys, it would have been more natural to simply release it as a toy. Looking back now, one could say "Nintendo was already considering an advance into the game industry even then," but that would be reading far too much into it with hindsight. After all, Yokoi himself said he had no idea what the true intent behind that remark was. The real meaning remains completely shrouded in mystery, but when you consider what Nintendo became and the work Yokoi went on to do, it is an episode laden with meaning.

The Ultra Hand's Prototype Was a "time-killing toy."

The "Ultra Hand" started out as something I made by connecting pieces of square lumber I bought at a hobby shop back in middle school.

It was fun that it extended and retracted, so I was just making it myself and playing with it. The extending part was what made it interesting, you know. Something that had been folded up would suddenly stretch right out. The kids around me were amazed too. You know how in old animated films, a robot's belly would open up and a fist would punch out? Things like that were probably an unconscious influence on me.

Something this simple could be easily made if you had a lathe, so I made one at the company and was playing around with it. Then the president saw it and told me to turn it into a product. It was the first time the president had ever seen something an employee was playing with and said, "Turn it into a product." When I was called in, I was completely sure I was going to get chewed out.

"Nintendo Is a Game Maker, So Make It a Game"

At that point, the president told me, "Nintendo is a game maker, so make it a game." But there's no way something like that could be a game. All it does is extend and retract. I agonized quite a bit over how on earth I could turn this into a game.

Around that time, Nintendo's main products were things like hanafuda cards, and "Yakuman" mahjong tiles were just starting to sell well. The company hadn't yet ventured into the world of toys or games. So I didn't really understand why the president said "Make it a game." I couldn't grasp his intent at all. Personally, I thought it was fine as just a simple tool.

Well, the president is the type who immediately thinks about television advertising, so there's no way to make a TV commercial out of a simple tool. That may be why he said "Make it a game." The president always used to say, "In America, they first decide on the storyboard for the television commercial, and then they move into product development." In other words, how effectively you can appeal to people is what's most important, apparently.

What's interesting about the Ultra Hand's mechanism is the structure that "grabs something far away and releases it close at hand." This mechanism is achieved using gears and a one-way clutch. When you extend the Ultra Hand, the "hand" part at the tip naturally narrows. This is a natural movement. Furthermore, the one-way clutch slides over the gear, preventing the "hand" part from opening. When the Ultra Hand is fully extended, the hand part is at its narrowest, and since it can't open, the clutch is holding it in place. In this state, if you retract the Ultra Hand, the clutch keeps the "hand" part from opening, so you can grip an object and pull it back toward you.

The problem was that after pulling it back to hand, you needed to be able to open the "hand." Initially, Yokoi had been thinking of a method where you would just pry it open by force with your fingers, but that wasn't very elegant, so he devised a further improvement. He ran a cord from the clutch back to the handle end, creating a mechanism where pulling the cord would release the clutch. In practice, you would squeeze the cord into the handle grip and extend the Ultra Hand just slightly. This would pull the cord taut, causing the hand part to open.

With the addition of this "grip it, then release it at your end" mechanism, the "Ultra Hand as a game" was born. It became a game where you pick up a ping-pong ball from a distance and transfer it to a platform in another location. However, based on my own memories of playing with the Ultra Hand at the time, I barely noticed the game aspect at all. What captivated me was the sensation — a blend of "laziness" and "convenience" — of being able to grab something far away, combined with the sheer fun of watching the clutch click-click-click as the arm extended outward.

In fact, these two qualities — "laziness + convenience" and "the fun of movement" — are essentially the Yokoi mind itself, and moreover, they are something common to every one of his subsequent works. "A debut work contains everything" is a well-known saying, and it applies perfectly to the Ultra Hand, Yokoi's own debut creation.

Grip-and-Release Mechanism

"The first thing I came up with was a game where you pick up colored ping-pong ball-like things and place them on platforms of the matching color. So the Ultra Hand had to be able to grab something at the extended end and release it freely wherever you wanted. That meant I had to devise all sorts of things for the tip."

"What I came up with was this: I ran a cord from the tip back toward the handle, and hung a weighted medal from it. When you retract the hand, the weight of that medal causes the cord to go slack. You grab that cord and push it forward just a little, and — pop — it opens up. That's the mechanism I devised. Nowadays you could do it much more elegantly, The Ultra Hand mechanism — uses a one-way clutch so that even when retracted and pulled back toward the user, it doesn't open. but back then I had no idea what to do — I was completely in the dark. After all, here was an electrical engineer making a mechanical toy. I was practically an amateur.

The Fun of "Movement" — Extending and Retracting

Without the ability to grab and release things, the Ultra Hand wouldn't exactly be called practical, but it would still feel somehow unsatisfying. So, to see just how far I could push it, I once tried placing a cigarette and a match far away, then using the Ultra Hand to grab the cigarette, bring it to my mouth, grab a single match, strike it, and light the cigarette. It took me five or ten minutes, though. A true virtuoso performance, if I do say so myself! (Laughs)

Quite a few knockoffs of the Ultra Hand appeared. But some of the knockoffs actually had more sophisticated mechanisms than the original, which was really something. After that, there were ones that didn't extend and retract, but simply gripped and grabbed things — those came out later. In practical terms, those were actually easier to use, I thought. But even though it seems like nothing special, the "movement" of extending and retracting is what makes the Ultra Hand fun. After all, the Ultra Hand is only truly the Ultra Hand when it extends and retracts.

Ultra Machine Ultra Machine

1968 (Showa 43). Price at time of release: ¥1,480

The Ultra Hand became a massive hit product. Along with that, Yokoi was freed from his equipment maintenance duties and transferred to the newly established development section. This was the start of what would become Nintendo's development department. But it was here that Yokoi began to feel enormous pressure. The Ultra Hand was, after all, a hit product that had come about by sheer luck — a bolt from the blue. From now on, he would have to consciously produce hit products. What's more, the success of the Ultra Hand led people within the company to overestimate him, saying things like, "As you'd expect from someone with a university degree — he's a cut above." "Having been rated above my actual abilities, I threw myself into studying from that point on, determined not to let anyone down." It was from here that Yokoi's true career as a developer truly began.

The Ultra Hand had followed the pattern of commercializing "a toy that he himself found fun," and the world ended up enjoying it too. However, this time Yokoi deliberately took on the challenge of commercializing "something that other people found fun, rather than something he enjoyed himself." At the time, Yokoi was completely hopeless at baseball, but he recalled the ping-pong baseball that childhood friends had been crazy about, and set his sights on commercializing it. This gamble paid off, and the "Ultra Machine" became an even bigger hit than the Ultra Hand.

If they were that absorbed in it, it must be fun

The Ultra Hand was something I made for my own amusement that then got commercialized, but the "Ultra Machine" was something I made with the goal of commercializing it from the start. The product concept was clear from the beginning — "a tool for practicing batting inside the house" — and that's how it was made.

When I made the Ultra Machine, the truth is I didn't know the first thing about baseball — in fact, I was more of a baseball hater. In middle school and high school, I had a wealthy friend — a bad influence — who could get any toy he wanted. But no matter when I went over to his place, all he ever did was have people throw ping-pong balls at him so he could hit them with a bamboo ruler. I couldn't understand why that was any fun when he had so many toys. It really stuck with me.

When I joined Nintendo and made the Ultra Hand, the next time I was told "Think of something," I remembered that. If that guy was that absorbed in it, it had to be fun. So I decided to try making a pitching machine that could be used inside a room.

Using a Real Pitching Machine as Reference

So I went to a batting center to see what kind of mechanism they used. It turned out they used a method where an arm rotates to throw the ball, so I used that as my reference. And it just so happened that there were ready-made balls like ping-pong balls available, so I decided to make something that could throw those.

When I looked at an actual pitching machine, it threw beautifully. To throw that well, I thought I'd need quite good control. I was really surprised when the first sample had excellent control. When I threw the ball and it hit right in the hole in the target display, I was thrilled. "Make a display for exhibitions," they told me, so I made a display where the ball would hit the target, drop into a hole, and cycle back to your hand — a nice little loop. But it wasn't easy to pull off, was it.

The managing director at the time thought it was cute and brought it around everywhere, but partway through, dents started forming in the ball, and the Ultra Machine started throwing curves too! "All it does is throw and it curves" — so we decided to adopt a ball that would curve, and we used that for the Ultra Machine.

When it came time to sell it, the manager who barely knew anything about baseball had somehow become a Giants fan along the way, so he wanted to watch the Giants' night games on TV (laughs). That led to doing a TV commercial during a Giants night game broadcast. That's when I got to meet Shigeo Nagashima, and we had a great laugh telling him this story.

Officially, this Ultra Machine was Yokoi's second product, but in fact the Ultra Drive Game (1966) Machine and Ultra Hand were preceded by a now-phantom "Home Drive Game" that had been commercialized. The mechanism was the same as the drive games found in department store rooftop arcades at the time — you steered a car with a steering wheel, and passing through set points earned you a score. Yokoi himself had come up with the concept and commercialized it on his own, but he had long forgotten about it. During the interview, Yokoi himself recalled it.

This Drive Game was, in the end, a failure. They tried to mass-produce it but couldn't — defective products kept coming off the line. Eventually Yokoi himself joined the assembly line, and while things sort of worked when he put them together, on the production line they didn't run properly. The mechanism was too finicky, and assembly work on the line simply couldn't keep up. With this single failure, Yokoi learned that "commercializing a product is a completely separate challenge from whether the game itself is fun." He came to understand the difficulties involved in mass production — that it required a different dimension of consideration from just saying "let's make it into a product."

The game itself received generally positive reviews from those around him, and while it was deemed a failure as a commercial product, production was halted, much to the regret of those involved. People praised it highly, saying it was a "great idea." From this experience, Yokoi took away the lesson that "a fun idea an interesting idea can be turned into a mass-produced product" — and from here he began paying serious attention to the question of how to make that happen. This is perhaps the true origin of why Yokoi's creations never ended up as mere novelty items.

The Difficulty of Product Packaging

The bat for the Ultra Machine was designed to extend and retract like a rod antenna4. "If you left the bat at its full length, the package would get too big, and you'd end up shipping air during transport — it's wasteful," I was told repeatedly. So with that in mind, I made the main unit an assembly-type design as well, and moreover one that a layperson could easily put together. This kind of challenge was an enormous burden. I'd never done or even thought about anything like that before.

I designed the main unit as a kit that would fit compactly inside the box, but when you actually assembled it, it was still unstable. In the end, I had to add screw-fastening sections and so on — it was a continuous process of trial and error. After that, I found that even in its fully assembled form, the package wouldn't actually be that much larger, and Ultra Machine box packaging I realized that for the second-generation Ultra Machine, I should design it in a pre-assembled form from the start. Looking back now, I feel like I went to an awful lot of trouble over something quite pointless.

My Toys Flowing Down a Conveyor Belt...

Around the time I joined Nintendo, there was a subsidiary called Sankin Shokuhin5, and they were making things like "Instant Rice," "Popeye Ramen," and a furikake seasoning called "Disney Flicker." But sales weren't very good.

Right after joining the company, I went to visit the Sankin Shokuhin factory, and I started dreaming: what if we could make toys in a factory like this, with the toys I designed flowing down a conveyor belt...

And then, with the Ultra Machine, that's exactly what happened. I was overjoyed — just thrilled beyond words. A department like a manufacturing division was established, conveyor belts were running, products were being packaged, and truck after truck was being loaded up and heading out. With the toys I had designed. It was a magnificent sight.

From around that point, I gained the confidence that the things I came up with would be accepted by the world.

Love Tester Love Tester

1969 (Showa 44). Price at time of release: ¥1,800

Having produced consecutive hits with the Ultra Hand and the Ultra Machine, Yokoi did an about-face and began thinking about commercializing "things he himself wanted." His confidence that "if I find something interesting, other people are sure to find it interesting too" had been validated by his string of hits. And here, another pillar of the Yokoi method of invention makes its appearance: the concept known as "Lateral Thinking with Seasoned Technology."

The Love Tester is, to be honest, nothing more than an ammeter. If you simply used it as a device for measuring electrical current, there would be nothing interesting about it at all. So Yokoi repackaged it as "a tool for openly holding a girl's hand." In other words, this is where the formula was born: take a well-worn technology like an ammeter and toss it into a completely unrelated field to see what new ideas emerge.

Products made using cutting-edge technology naturally come with high costs. What's more, the products born from such technology tend to end up in price wars with competitors. However, once a technology becomes widespread and fully matures, products can be manufactured at astonishingly low cost. The trick is then to add a twist to how that technology is used and turn it into a product. This is the Yokoi method of invention — "Lateral Thinking with Seasoned Technology." The Love Tester6 would become the first toy to put this concept into practice.

A Toy for Holding a Girl's Hand

Since I majored in electrical engineering, I felt like I ought to do something electronic or it wouldn't look right. But I couldn't really do anything too difficult. One day, I happened to be playing around with a tester set to the resistance range, and it seemed like electrical current was flowing through the human body. I wondered if I could use this as a way to hold a girl's hand — and that's where the idea for the "Love Tester" came from. I stuck a single transistor together with a cheap galvanometer I'd bought, and since it was a love test, I made the units things like 1 Love, 2 Love — it was really a slapdash thing.

Love Tester mechanism diagram The Love Tester targeted a higher age group than the Ultra Series, didn't it. The Ultra Hand and Ultra Machine were for elementary and middle school kids. But the Love Tester was really for middle schoolers and up. Around this time, I think I started wanting to make things that I myself would find fun.

I heard that when middle school kids came to buy one, it was like buying one of those vinyl-wrapped7 books you see today — they were embarrassed about it. Nowadays it wouldn't be anything at all, of course.

I myself used the Love Tester to hold quite a few women's hands. Well, eventually that alone wasn't enough anymore, though. (Laughs)

This is a digression, but since there's an amusing episode related to this, let me share it. Yokoi describes the Love Tester as "a really half-baked thing," but this is typical Yokoi-style modesty. In principle, it works on exactly the same mechanism as a modern polygraph (lie detector). When a man and woman with strong feelings for each other hold hands, they naturally sweat on their palms, which makes the current flow more easily. Theoretically, it's a perfectly sound device.

Furthermore, according to Yokoi, "Kissing produces more moisture than just holding hands, so the love reading should come out higher." Apparently the pamphlet at the time even included a description to that effect. However, a supervisor who was supposed to be out of the office called the company and said in a dead-serious, angry voice, "Hey! The needle doesn't move any differently even when we kiss!" Everyone burst out laughing, wondering where on earth this supervisor — who was supposed to be out on business — was, and what exactly he was doing. That's how much the Love Tester had elements that could captivate even full-grown adults.

Perhaps because the impact of the Love Tester as "a machine that measures love" was so strong, it reportedly remains a collector's item to this day.

Ele-Conga Eleconga

1970 (Showa 45) — Original retail price: ¥9,800

When people think of Yokoi, if they judge solely from the nature of his work and the photos that occasionally appear in the media, most would probably get the impression that he was "a straightforward, earnest sort of person." And certainly there is no doubt that he was a sincere individual — but in reality, he was also a man of refined tastes with a genuine passion for music and cars. He had studied piano since childhood, competed in ballroom dancing competitions during his student days, driven foreign cars, and enjoyed diving in the summer — by all accounts, he was something of a "sophisticated *taiyōzoku* type" — one of those sun-loving, pleasure-seeking young men of the era. A surprising number of Yokoi's creations drew on the sensibilities of that period in his life. The Light Gun, for instance, originated from the underwater spear guns he played with as a student. And this "Eleconga" is another such example.

In a word, the Eleconga was essentially today's rhythm box8. It was a toy that, when you struck the keys, produced the corresponding percussion instrument sounds as electronic tones. Later, by punching holes in paper you could also have it play automatically with an adapter. At the time of release, it wasn't quite a hit product, but it was extremely popular among hobbyist musicians. It could be enjoyed as a toy, and it also had the practical utility of being usable as a rhythm box when you were actually playing guitar or other instruments yourself.

Can One Person Produce Percussion9 Sounds Alone?

The sound of percussion instruments really does capture people's emotions. Latin percussion in particular — I had heard for a long time that it resonates incredibly well with Japanese people. I myself had studied piano from elementary school and was well versed in music, so I wondered whether there was some way to produce percussion sounds by yourself.

In ordinary Latin music, the conga, bongo, güiro, and so on are each played by a separate person, but the sound is easy to replicate with electronic sounds. I had been wondering whether one person could perform all of them alone, and then I noticed that Yamaha's Electone had keys that could produce percussion sounds. I wondered if this could be turned into a product, and what I made was the "Eleconga." I had always thought that music-related products were difficult to sell commercially, but I wondered just how far it might go, so I made it based on my own personal interest.

The problem was, it turned out that if you couldn't play piano, you couldn't play it at all. To perform a proper rhythm, you had to use all five fingers to the fullest. I could play piano, so it was fine for me, but I realized that other people simply couldn't play it.

This was a problem — I had to make it so that anyone could play it.

So I added a mechanism where you punch holes in a disc, one revolution equals 32 beats, and it plays automatically. Nowadays you'd call it a rhythm box. People who wanted to play it themselves could use the keyboard, and those who found that difficult could use the auto-play machine. It also came with a hole punch, so you could create your own rhythms.

Normally, music isn't music without melody. The idea of making music with percussion sounds alone is a fairly recent concept, I suppose. Back then, people who were involved in music were quite Ele-Conga product box interested in it, and they were the ones saying "I want one," you know.

N&B Block Crater N&B Block Crater

1970 (Showa 45). Price at time of release: ¥1,800

Since Yokoi was naturally a Nintendo employee, all manner of work came his way beyond just the projects he himself had planned. The Famicom is one such example. The Famicom itself was being developed in a department separate from Yokoi's. However, when it came to the design of the housing and keypad, the request came to Yokoi's department. This "N&B Block Crater" was also one of those commissioned jobs — the blocks themselves were made by a completely separate department from Yokoi. What Yokoi was involved with were the parts that accompanied the blocks.

What makes this Crater so delightful is the reverse thinking it contains. Normally, when you think of blocks, you naturally think of assembling them into some kind of shape. However, the Crater is a contraption that launches the blocks you've painstakingly built, scattering them everywhere. And it was a hit. While it didn't quite reach the level of a mega-hit, it was apparently a solid hit product with brisk sales.

If you watch children playing with blocks closely, you'll see that building shapes is really just a preliminary stage. Many children actually take more pleasure in destroying what they've built. As a product, it may not be one that sticks in people's memories, but it can certainly be said to be one of the things where Yokoi's playful spirit was put to good use.

The Pleasure of Watching Something Built Fall Apart

Nintendo once imitated Lego and made their own blocks. It even led to lawsuits — there was a whole lot going on with that.

I myself wasn't directly involved with those blocks, but a plastics molding company brought in a proposal, and when it came time to sell them in various combinations, the block N&B Block Crater packaging Since blocks are all about building things, I thought it would be interesting to flip that idea on its head — what if you could destroy what you'd built? So if you stepped on something like a landmine, the blocks you'd painstakingly assembled would fly apart. That sounded fun, so the order came right away: make that landmine. Since the shape resembled a crater on the moon's surface, we released it under the product name "Crater."

The challenge with this mechanism was that it had to send something quite heavy flying. You had to make heavy objects scatter with just a light impact. That was the tricky part. It actually worked quite well, so we got a taste for it, and in fact we used this same mechanism in the Light Gun "Bottle." A coil spring held it in place, and when the Light Gun's beam hit the bottle, it would burst into the air. That one was well received too.

Lefty RX radio-controlled car Lefty RX

1972 (Showa 47). Price at time of release: ¥5,900–¥12,900

Yokoi may appear to have been releasing unique toys into the world with no connection whatsoever to the trends of the times, but of course that's not the case. He developed a considerable number of toys by observing the booms sweeping society and adding fresh ideas of his own. This "Lefty RX" was one such toy.

The radio-controlled car boom that began in the early 1970s continues to enjoy strong popularity even today. The problem, however, is that RC toys remain expensive even now. They are hardly the kind of thing a child can buy with pocket money. In fact, the age group currently enjoying RC as a hobby is almost entirely adults. RC is seen as a pastime one can enjoy from age twenty all the way into retirement — deep, technically advanced, and more than anything, the simple fact that "it costs money." Back in the 1970s, radio-controlled toys were an even more expensive proposition. If you wanted to buy one, you had to be prepared to spend roughly a month's salary.

The reason RC gets so expensive comes down mainly to the control channels. In the case of a car, for example, you need at minimum two channels — one to control engine output and one to control steering. If you want to improve operability, even more channels become necessary. Move to airplanes or helicopters, and the required number of channels increases further still.

This is where the "Lefty RX" entered the picture. Its distinguishing feature was that it could only turn left — but because it was controlled with just a single channel, the cost could be kept extremely low. And the real trick was that even though it could only turn left, you could still hold a proper race with it.

Controlling an RC Car with One Channel

At the time, there were toy cars equipped with nickel-cadmium10 batteries that you could rapid-charge from ordinary dry-cell batteries and then run. The idea was, "Let's turn this into a radio-controlled car." It was Nintendo's very first foray into RC.

The problem was that RC involved enormous costs per channel. Couldn't we somehow reduce the number of channels and create an RC car that people could race freely? That was the thinking behind the "Lefty RX."

At the time, there was an RC boom. However, multichannel units were extremely expensive. If you were willing to spend the money, there were plenty of technically excellent products out there, but we felt strongly that we wanted to bring what existed in the hobby world into the toy world as well. A proper RC car cost more than ten times what the Lefty did. The Lefty can only turn left, but with just one channel you can control everything.

So a left-turning course poses no problem at all. Even if it hits a wall, it turns left and Lefty RX packaging starts running again. It's limited, to be sure — but the selling point was the low price. At the time, at any rate, the idea that you could race at this price was simply unthinkable.

Time Shock Time Shock

1972 (Showa 47). Price at time of release: ¥1,800

Yokoi doesn't have much to say about this "Time Shock." The reason is that it was a product developed to compete against a rival company's offering. At the time, a puzzle game called "Perfection" — in which you inserted variously shaped blocks into their corresponding holes, and when time ran out, a spring mechanism would send the blocks flying out with a snap — was selling extremely well. What was developed to compete against it was Time Shock. Conversely, there were also many cases where Yokoi's own creations were imitated by others, and knockoffs of the Ultra Hand, Ultra Machine, and Game & Watch in particular flooded the market in huge quantities. Competition in the toy industry was fierce even back then.

When you spin the disc, the shape of the holes changes

"Time Shock" — I'd rather not talk about it too much, but honestly, it was an imitation. Of "Perfection." Perfection was selling so well that it was frustrating, so we wanted to see if we could come up with something to compete.

The distinguishing feature of Time Shock is that when you spin the disc, the shape of the holes changes. At the time, there was a popular TV show called "Time Shock," so we borrowed the name from that. Well, even among my own work, this was about as half-hearted as it gets.

With games, you can really only register designs or trademarks. So with the Ultra Hand, for example, blatant copies flooded the market, and it got to the point where we couldn't even tell who was making them. Nintendo itself didn't have a proper legal framework in place either. Even if we had filed a lawsuit, we probably wouldn't have won.

That kind of legal infrastructure really started coming together around the time of Game & Watch, I'd say. Back then, Nintendo wasn't a company that attracted all that much attention.

Chiritori with product box Chiritori

1979 (Showa 54). Price at release: ¥5,800

If you had to sum up Yokoi in a single phrase, it would be as an inventor of toys. What's interesting, however, is that he surprisingly harbored an admiration for "practical goods." For example, the early Ultra Hand had the practical appeal of being able to "grab things far away," and the Ultra Scope (a toy modeled on a periscope, designed for peeking over tall walls and fences; 1971) was built on a balance between playful appeal — golf spectating — and practical utility. That balance was most fully realized in the Game Boy, which came later. The Game Boy's secret to success lay not only in its playful appeal as a portable game system, but also in its practical side — the fact that it could run for long hours on AA batteries.

After making the one-channel radio-controlled Lefty RX, Yokoi created the "Chiritori" Chiritori mechanism diagram — a radio-controlled vacuum cleaner. More than a toy, it was closer to a practical product with a sense of playfulness.

Using the One-Channel Radio Control for Something Practical

"With the Chiritori, I applied the one-channel radio control from the Lefty and tried to think of a way to do some kind of practical work with it. There was a manga in the newspaper that featured mounting a vacuum cleaner on a radio-controlled car. We laughed about it at the company, saying, 'Everyone thinks of the same things, don't they?' It happened to come out right when we were making the Chiritori. It's a radio-controlled toy, but one that can also be used for practical purposes."

Ten Billion puzzle Ten Billion

1980 (Showa 55). Price at time of release: ¥1,000

As many readers will remember, the "Rubik's Cube" released by Tsukuda Original in the summer of 1980 became a massive craze. Even today, footage of young people enjoying the Rubik's Cube is frequently used to evoke the culture of that era. The Rubik's Cube spawned all manner of imitations and knockoff products, and before long, the Rubik's Cube boom transformed into a broader puzzle boom. The product that played a leading role in driving that shift was the "Ten Billion."

The Ten Billion was, of course, a puzzle game born out of the inspiration of the Rubik's Cube. Yokoi himself described it as "something I did on the side, almost as an afterthought." However, what is not widely known is that it was the next puzzle to become a hit after the Rubik's Cube — and not just domestically, but as a worldwide hit product. Among mathematicians and puzzle fans Ten Billion packaging it was also highly popular, and books explaining how to solve the Ten Billion were even published.

What is particularly interesting is that Yokoi himself, the inventor, had absolutely no idea how to solve it. His job was, first and last, to create an interesting mechanism — nothing more.

As an aside, in a German book that introduced the Ten Billion (page 62), Yokoi is described as "the discoverer of the Devil's Stone." "Devil's Stone" is the product name under which the Ten Billion was sold in Germany. What is interesting is that he is introduced not as the inventor but as the discoverer. There is a theory that this is simply a mistranslation or typo, but one could also interpret it as follows: "The mechanism known as the Devil's Stone was something prepared by God from the very beginning, and a man named Yokoi was chosen by God and entrusted with the mission of discovering it." Yokoi did indeed discover the mechanism that is the Ten Billion — he did not invent it with full knowledge of how to play it or how to solve it. Even if it is just a typo, it makes for a rather amusing story.

"If you sort the colors and put them in, it'll go back to the way it was"

The Ten Billion is, frankly speaking, an imitation of the Rubik's Cube. I pretty much knocked it out as a side project. But the person who made it didn't know how to solve it. There were times when customers taught me how to solve it and I was genuinely impressed (laughs).

In any case, I made it as a puzzle where you put balls in and match colors. When I thought about the mechanism for rearranging the colors, it naturally ended up becoming a cylindrical shape. While I was making it, I decided that bulging out the middle would look cooler, so it became barrel-shaped.

The Rubik's Cube is a square puzzle, right? But the mechanism of the Rubik's Cube is extremely difficult. With balls, it's simple. The idea of a puzzle where you spin them around to line them up vertically naturally led to a cylindrical shape.

What made the Rubik's Cube a hit, I think, was less the fun of the puzzle itself and more the wonder of its mechanism. "How is it that you can spin the squares around and around and they don't fall apart?"

That's what makes it so fascinating, I think. At the time, I was busy with Game & Watch, so I figured that riding the Rubik's Cube craze with just a color-matching puzzle would be good enough, and I ordered my subordinates to plan and develop it.

But then they said to me, "How do you solve this thing?" And I said, "I don't know either. When you sell it, just put the colors in sorted. Even if the customer scrambles it all up, eventually it'll definitely go back to the original arrangement." Pretty irresponsible, right? Then customers started saying, "Teach me how to solve it." That was a real problem. Fortunately, another customer figured out the solution and taught it to us. Then I pretended I'd worked out the solution myself and even went on television and everything (laughs).

The number of combinations is actually far greater than ten billion!?

The name "Ten Billion" came about because the Rubik's Cube had something like one hundred million or two hundred million combinations, so someone said, "Let's calculate the number of combinations." And when we did, it turned out to be so many that you could barely calculate it. Trillions and beyond — that sort of order of magnitude. So, German magazine article about Ten Billion we couldn't very well go with that, so we decided to round it down to ten billion. We were reducing it, you see. So we called it "Ten Billion." For such a throwaway name, it has a surprisingly nice ring to it, don't you think?
