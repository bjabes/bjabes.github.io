Game & Watch not only sparked a handheld game boom in Japan, but went on to become an explosive hit worldwide — not merely a liquid-crystal novelty, but a genuine phenomenon. In its wake, for roughly the next ten years, Yokoi found himself perpetually consumed with Game & Watch work. During that period, he steadily accumulated assets, which he then channeled into developing the Famicom — and so Game & Watch gave way to a new era. In the 1980s, Nintendo in Kyoto transformed into "Nintendo of the World." When the Game & Watch boom began to subside, it was Yokoi who stepped up next to develop one game after another into hits. Game & Watch is fundamentally a standalone device — each unit plays only one game. Its multi-software format is the Game Boy. If you want to enjoy a different game, you simply need another Game & Watch unit; but if you want to enjoy a different game on Game Boy, you just need ano p.148 ther cartridge. Of course, as long as you have the unit itself, you can play any number of games — that's what multi-software means. The cost issue was naturally the biggest concern from the user side, and this is what made it such a strong point as a product.

At the time, however, Nintendo had already launched the Famicom and it was enjoying enormous popularity. Looking at it from the design perspective, the internals were almost identical to the Famicom. But the multi-software version of Game & Watch uses a liquid-crystal monitor — it can't hold a candle to the Famicom. Yet on the pocket-gaming front, it costs more than the Famicom. This is precisely why the multi-software version of Game & Watch was never developed for the Famicom. The world simply wouldn't accept it at a price higher than the Famicom. However, selling it at a lower price than the Famicom became the ultimate challenge for its development. This was the biggest problem — "Would it sell as a Famicom alternative?" — and it triggered the response: "Sell the multi-software version of Game & Watch at a lower price than the Famicom." This became the supreme development challenge.

p.149 No matter how you crunch the numbers, the price just doesn't work out

Once Game & Watch dropped down a tier to become the Game Boy — making it multi-software capable (meaning you could swap out the software) — both of those things had already been decided in my mind. The concept had to be priced below the Famicom. The problem was that it needed to be below the Famicom's price, and it was something nobody could agree on. "That's just the way it is," you might say. But the multi-software approach itself was something nobody could accept — that's what it amounted to.

The display is monochrome. And it's multi-software. Yet the price is impossible to achieve. So no matter how cheap you make it, the screen is monochrome and that's a deal-breaker, which means all the features that can be cut should be cut. But it has to be a monochrome display — that's non-negotiable.

With that in mind, we started competing on chip-on-glass technology. That turned out to be interesting — a technique had been found where you could burn circuits directly onto a liquid-crystal screen. This was a technology called chip-on-glass. Using this brought costs down considerably. And once we had that, it looked like we could finally reach the price we'd been targeting all along.

p.150 The problem, however, was that Sharp simply couldn't make the numbers work on the liquid crystal side. For their part, Sharp was relying on entirely new entrants to the liquid crystal world, and had been through more than a year of trial and error trying to bring the price down. Meanwhile, the Game Boy team was trying to negotiate prices with Sharp — or switch to Citizen, or have Sharp come down, or have Citizen step in. When it came down to it, once we made up our minds to go with Citizen, Sharp suddenly made it real. They came around quickly once Citizen entered the picture, you see. And just like that, the Game Boy was able to come together.

On the software side, things were far from ideal — we somehow managed to get to the point of commercializing the multi-software Game & Watch, the "Game Boy," as it were. But at one point, Yokoi himself committed a grave mistake. "That was the biggest blunder of my life," and "It's the kind of thing that, socially speaking, Yokoi couldn't have even imagined saying" — those words began flying around at the time, seriously, to the point of him wanting to disappear.

The thing about liquid crystal displays is that they show beautiful images from any angle — though that's not actually true. LCD screens don't look perfectly clear from every direction. Take word processors and laptop computers, for instance — they're used p.151 at an angle, and the screen suddenly goes dark — that's a key point anyone who's worked with LCD monitors knows all too well.

Given that, the question for whoever wants to build an LCD product is: at what angle do you expect the user to hold it? In the case of Game & Watch, since you happened to be holding it in the palm of your hand, the display was made to look best from a slightly downward angle — and as it turned out, that worked just fine. That's why the liquid crystal used in Game & Watch was adopted in that form.

The ideal viewing angle for playing a game is about five degrees below horizontal. Compare that to Game & Watch, and the Game Boy's body is considerably larger. What this means is that when you look at only the LCD portion of the Game Boy, you end up peering at it from slightly above. This became a significant problem for the Game Boy development team. By the time they realized it, the trial production phase was already underway, and Yokoi himself found himself deeply troubled.

p.152 That five-degree angle is a major blind spot when it comes to the TN liquid crystal used in desktop calculators. When you use a calculator, the display is designed to look best from straight on — and when viewed from about five degrees below horizontal, the contrast actually looks quite good. But with the Game Boy, since the screen faces straight ahead, the contrast turns out poor. The Game Boy was built to prevent reflections from indoor lighting and sunlight from washing out the image, so it ended up being viewed from slightly above. Sharp managed to produce prototype units that could be seen from a somewhat higher angle, and when we saw them we thought, "Ah, this'll work."

But then, although it worked fine at the time, we somehow drifted back to viewing it at five degrees below horizontal without really noticing. Nintendo is a pretty small company, you know. So the division heads at Nintendo came along with Game & Watch and said things like, "OK" — and by "OK," they meant it in the biggest possible sense. After that, it turned out that Sharp had spent four billion yen building a dedicated factory, and the moment I said "OK" — p.153 the president came to look at the prototype and said, "What's this? I can't see a thing." And indeed, when you looked at it straight on, the screen really wasn't visible at all. The president said, "What are we supposed to do with this? We can't sell this thing" — and just like that, he went silent.

If it hadn't been saved at that moment, I would have killed myself

At that point I was in terrible trouble. I had a very strong sense that I couldn't let our partner companies down. It's because of our partners that things get made, after all — I was always conscious of that. So I couldn't just tell them, "Forget it," just like that. I felt I couldn't betray the trust they had placed in me.

But the thing was, Sharp had already built a dedicated factory with four billion yen of investment, and the credit I had built up since joining Nintendo was on the verge of going to zero — or so I thought. At that point, I found myself stranded in the middle of nowhere with an STN liquid crystal display that had terrible contrast and no one to turn to. And then — "this p.154 is terribly slow, and in the game you can't see anything moving. It's completely useless. And since there's no other way to do it either — just try it once more," is what Sharp told me. But no matter how many times you try, it's no good. So for the next six months, I stopped eating meals and everything. My brother-in-law is a doctor, and even he was worried about me. He said, "Get a blood test," so I did, and the results showed nutritional deficiency. I had even considered suicide. I couldn't eat, couldn't sleep, couldn't hear anything anyone said to me anymore. Someone would say something to me and I'd go completely blank — "I just can't take this in."

During that time I went to the Sharp factory. After that, the Chinese restaurant there was incredibly kind to me — the food was delicious, and somehow it lifted my spirits enormously. Something about the Chinese food there, I don't know what it was, but it saved me completely. And then a Sharp salesman called and said, "We've managed it, you know. It's the STN liquid crystal. Data from that — well, it turned out we accidentally got it working, but the technical team, when I called them, said 'We got it working but the data's probably been taken away somewhere mid-experiment, and a salesman happened to see it, so p.155 the data showed it actually displayed reasonably well.' So after that, it was a week of hope and anxiety. Somehow it seemed to be displaying properly. So prioritizing speed, with speed and contrast being the issue — if you measured the actual contrast values, contrast did drop, but in terms of human perception, it didn't affect the human eye that much. The balance of contrast and speed for the human eye — the data on that was something we hadn't really given much thought to. Contrast, well, as long as it's usable, that would do. So I flew over to the president's office with a prototype sample. I boldly brought the prototype in and showed it to him, and he said, "Ah, so you've pulled it off then," and that was all — (laughs) the president just looked at it and said, "Ah, this'll do," and it turned out it wasn't quite the enormous problem I'd thought it was.

p.156 The Game Boy that was thus born went on to become a popular toy over more than a decade, remaining a long-selling dedicated handheld device capable of displaying only monochrome. What makes it unique is that it can only display monochrome. Users strongly requested "give us color," but it could not be done. The reason is that it was the first-generation Game Boy. Nine years after its release, the Game Boy Color appeared — but the cost of making it color was high, and battery life would be shorter. Beyond that, Yokoi himself chose monochrome for reasons deeply rooted in his game philosophy. He never felt enthusiastic about color. In other words, when it comes to games, things like display content, processing speed, color, polygon graphics, and so forth — Yokoi says, "After this, he remarked, 'I don't really feel like making games anymore,' and the reason is that, frankly, he felt that the games of today had exhausted their ideas. Beautiful graphics, sound, the fierce competition among the large-capacity titles currently on the market —

Yokoi's Reasons for Insisting on Monochrome

Around the time the Game Boy was being made, the Famicom was at the height of its popularity, and I kept being asked, "Why monochrome? Why not make a multi-software version of Game & Watch?" Personally, I had always centered my thinking on the concept of "portability." Naturally, it had to run on batteries. And color LCD displays at the time had a battery life of around one and a half hours — barely functional as a game device. On top of that, if you were walking around and playing for ten or even twenty hours, you'd need the battery to last. But color LCD screens couldn't be seen in bright outdoor light. And then there was the backlight issue — backlit LCD screens are fine indoors, but outdoors they're invisible. So color was out of the question.

Back in the day, the only real option was a monochrome LCD. Nobody at the company pushed back on that.

But then the president brought me a color LCD television — he was so pleased about it, gave it to me himself. So I used it, and after an hour it went dead. The battery died. I thought, that's a problem. What was the president thinking? And then I realized — that thing is a television, and even as a television it has issues. That's p.158 why even the engineers were surprised — "Can't you make it color?" they'd say. "Can't you use a color display?" If you make it color, then you need to use an AC adapter. But using an AC adapter means you can't play it on the go. Compared to the Famicom, the screen is small, and it has to be placed in a room connected to an AC adapter — that's the Famicom's drawback. So the president said, "The battery dies right away — that's a problem. Monochrome is fine." The screen is small, and compared to the Famicom you can't see it easily, and because it uses a different signal path from the Famicom it has to be demonstrated as a demo unit in the store, which makes it hard to understand, and the price goes up — the president said, "The battery dies right away, so monochrome is just fine." And so, bright areas are hard to see, and the acidic way people were saying "the price is too high" — through all of that, the company understood, and that settled it.

And then, knockoff products started appearing. This product is definitely going to get copied, I thought. And sure enough, knockoffs came out. And on top of that, they were color — and color ones were hugely popular (laughs). They really sold well. When I heard that story, I thought, "Good — that's fine. Good." That was Sega's "Game Gear." And around that time there was a really funny manga — the manga that compared Game Gear and Game Boy — and that was Sega's p.159 strategy — "I can rotate, enlarge, reduce, and scale freely — color is what I am!" That manga had Game Boy going "What happened to you?" When I heard that, the battery dying and the knockoffs — I was just happy about it. This manga perfectly captured what I was aiming for. It was posted right next to my desk.

Black snowmen look white too

The basics of gaming are things like Go and shogi — and no matter how much color you add to them, they don't really need it. In the end, color was the biggest drawback at first. And the fact that you can demonstrate it — at the time it was color — that didn't matter much either. The same thing happened when Sony released the transistor radio — when it first came out, it sold really well. But then the same thing happened: Noise was a big problem, and people were saying "Sony has a lot of noise." But the battery lasted a long time, so in the end, the transistor radio won out. The sound was better than vacuum tube radios, you see. But overall, the transistor radio was superior. Though, somehow — p.160 the initial impression is what matters for entertainment products — that's what determines how well they sell. Toys sell with a flashy "wow" factor, and after two or three hours of play people get bored and move on. But that's what toys are. Game Boy, though, is something you keep coming back to because of the software.

For me, practical strengths are what's most important. "Try drawing it" — even in monochrome, go ahead and draw a snowman. Black snowmen look white too, you see. Apples look properly red even in monochrome. Drawn in black, a highway tunnel — well, you don't really remember what it looks like anyway. But if you film it with video in black and white, it looks single-colored. As for old movies — they were color too, weren't they? People have taken color as a given and stopped really seeing it. Even so, apples do look properly red, and tunnels do look black. But filming with video in monochrome — it all just looks flat.

So when TV games started chasing all these millions of colors and things like that, I felt: this is a game machine moving in a completely different direction from what gaming is really about.

p.161 Can't we get back to the essence of gaming, just once more?

In the world of the Famicom, Game & Watch, and Game Boy, if you think about what lies beyond them, you find yourself thinking: I want to make brand-new games with all my might. If the other side is Go, then our side is shogi — that kind of attitude existed, you see.

The thing is, once you add color, a freshness emerges. But then that freshness fades, and you start thinking it's no good if things don't move. And once things are moving, TV games began to feel stale. So then the idea gets twisted around, and not only the idea but the speed increases too. This whole thing flows toward the easy route, with more and more elaborate effects added in. It keeps escalating — CPU going from 8 to 16, 32, 64 bits. Even as it all moves slowly, hitting dead ends, it still moves slowly. The thing is, "idea" is a matter of relativity, so ideas do emerge. But "ideas aren't coming out" — in TV games, that idea shortage is simply a shortage of ideas.

In the end, games are fundamentally about ideas — so "ideas aren't coming out" in TV games means that idea shortage is nothing more than a shortage of ideas. But then, p.162 there was always an escape route. That escape route was the CPU race, the color race. Once it comes to that, companies like Nintendo — whose forte is making games in the true sense — find themselves in an awkward position, and CG production becomes the thing everyone is good at. So then, Nintendo's position becomes untenable, I think. That's why we made something that once again returns to the essence of gaming — that was the Virtual Boy.

Game Boy Software

The Game Boy is essentially a multi-software version of the Game & Watch. Naturally, once development of the Game Boy hardware was complete, Yokoi threw himself into developing Game Boy software. In the case of the Game Boy, the software was not sent out by Nintendo alone — third parties also got involved, and most of them came to Yokoi in one form or another.

However, it became clear that the Game & Watch and the Game Boy have completely different natures as games. In Game & Watch and Game Boy titles, all the game data is burned into the screen in advance. For example, you draw the character's sprite art, and then animate it by lighting up several of those characters on the LCD screen in sequence from left to right — moving it left to right in that order creates the illusion of movement.

p.164 With the Game Boy, just as with modern personal computers, you can display anything you like on the screen. You can move a character around freely — just keep shifting it one dot at a time, and the character moves continuously. The Game Boy allows for smooth, continuous movement, whereas Game & Watch could only manage choppy, discrete motion. It is precisely this difference in the hardware that makes the Game & Watch's gameplay timing-based and nothing more, whereas the Game Boy opens up the fun of gaming itself, Mr. Yokoi says. "Game & Watch is deeply interesting in its own right." He felt this difference keenly.

To experience this difference for yourself, I'd encourage you to play the Game Boy software that Nintendo released. Among those titles are the "Game Boy Gallery" games — Nintendo remakes with original Game & Watch versions and Game Boy versions, two varieties you can enjoy. Comparing the two side by side, the difference between Game & Watch and Game Boy becomes immediately clear. You can find them on the used-software market, so I think it's well worth tracking one down — though playing it is by no means simple.

p.165 One more thing worth noting is that the Game Boy gave birth to ideas that are now taken for granted in today's gaming world. For example, competitive puzzle games, falling-block puzzle games, chain reactions — all of these ideas were born from the Game Boy. The falling-block genre's progenitor, "Tetris," is itself a game to which Yokoi's concept of falling pieces contributed, and the many varied ideas that were added to it gave rise to the history of falling-block games as we know them today. It is no exaggeration to say that Yokoi's work sits at the very heart of this genre's popularity.

Game & Watch and Game Boy Are Completely Different

Game & Watch software and Game Boy software inhabit entirely different worlds. Game & Watch moves each piece one frame at a time, digitally — it's a timing game, you see. Game & Watch is a game you read frame by frame. The Game Boy, on the other hand, is a game of continuous motion.

p.166 So when you port Game & Watch software to the Game Boy, the Game & Watch jumps around. It moves smoothly — that's because it reads timing like a game should. If you port it to the Game Boy as-is, the Game & Watch aspect becomes meaningless, you see. That's what makes Game Boy games come alive. With Game Boy software, almost all of it I was quite deeply involved in myself. To varying degrees, I offered suggestions and direction. Some of it, like the Pocket Monster concept, I haven't been involved with at all, though.

Competitive Tetris — The Program Took One Week

The Game Boy's first software titles were "Tennis," "Block Breaker," and "Mario Land." As for that first game, the Mario character was accepted with the understanding that anyone would play a side-scrolling game featuring Mario — that was the consensus. Among the early software titles, the one that really carried the Game Boy forward was "Tet- p.167 ris." By the time we were launching the Game Boy, the Famicom version of Tetris already existed, and we felt dissatisfied that it hadn't been taken up by us — we really wanted it. So I thought, how can we make this work? We asked one of our programmers to do it, and he finished the program in one week. The program itself was simple, you see. And then it was the link cable that brought it to life — that's what made competitive Tetris a hit for the first time. Competitive Tetris, as an idea, had existed before. Simply put, it's a race to clear blocks — but none of our programmers had thought of it. Born from the President's Instructions: "Dr. Mario"

At Nintendo, every year at the start of work in January, there's a New Year's ceremony lasting about an hour, where the president gives a speech. And in it, he always says something along the lines of, "Games — software — are what matter. No matter how good the company's performance is, his instructions on that point are always strict. Among those instructions, what he always stresses is: "Make something that will sell." As the person who made Game Boy software, that really hit home for me. Everything we put out on Game Boy, I felt the pressure — "It has to sell."

So I thought, well, if Tetris can fit together shapes, then anyone could come up with the idea of making colors match. If you can make them disappear in 4 colors, then ideas for 4-color matching will follow naturally. While we were playing around with all sorts of variations, it became too complicated to be a game anymore — it couldn't be done in 4 colors. Since 2 colors would be fine, and since 2 colors made you think of medicine capsules, it started to look like — that's what gave us "Doctor Mario." That's how it was made. But it was too hard, you see. And then, looking at it with 2 colors, the leaves became medicine capsules, and p.169 that became the concept of Dr. Mario. In Dr. Mario, when you clear blocks, the blocks above come falling down, and for the first time I realized that triggered chain reactions. So when I saw "Puyo Puyo," I thought it was a clear imitation. That's why when I saw it I said, "That's a blatant ripoff of mine," but apparently when we looked into it later, it seemed that might not have been the case. Though I honestly haven't verified that myself, and the matter isn't entirely settled. There were also stories going around at the time that "they copied me," but as for the truth — I haven't investigated it.

Dropped Pieces Make It Interesting — "Yoshi's Egg"

The Yoshi series was developed as both a Game Boy and a Famicom software title simultaneously. The idea was to avoid using color in the screen layout so that it could also be used on Game Boy. That's why it was designed to work on the Famicom as well. p.170 "Yoshi's Egg" was originally conceived as a game where you flip Yoshi's row upside down — that was the initial proposal brought to me. But the processing would drop frames on the CPU, and the screen redraws were just too slow to display properly. The movement inside was somehow neither interesting nor uninteresting. In short, the screen redraw was a fatal flaw for the software. So the game's essence — that "eggs should be born from inside, making it interesting" — became the concept, and "Yoshi's Egg" was decided as the name.

In the process of deciding what to call it, I came up with the name "Yoshi's Egg," and when I presented it to the company president, he said, "What? 'Yoshi's Egg'? That's a surprisingly fun name," and was impressed. But later he came around and said, "It's a good name, isn't it," and seemed satisfied.

According to Yokoi, he had long been thinking about leaving Nintendo. It wasn't that he had any particular dissatisfaction with Nintendo as a company — it was simply that he was already past fifty, and he wanted to enjoy the rest of his life doing only the work he truly loved, and to spend his remaining years in leisure. He said he had felt that way for some time. Meanwhile, the gaming world of that era was naturally dominated by the CPU wars — the battle over graphics performance. Nintendo, too, was being swept up in it, with the Super Famicom, the Nintendo 64, and their rivalries all drawing everyone into that whirlpool. Or rather, Nintendo was at the very center of that whirlpool, and

p.173 Yokoi himself came to feel that there was no longer a place for him within Nintendo.

Such was his thinking. Against this tide, Yokoi was not a man who competed. The current state of gaming was dominated by pushing graphics performance — using high-performance CPUs to overwhelm players with large-capacity discs and ROM cartridges. The prevailing scenario was one of massive scale. What Yokoi clearly belonged to was what one might call the guerrilla type — a type that simply doesn't fit the mainstream today.

Guerrilla warfare is, at its core, about winning through attrition. But Yokoi was plainly not the attrition type. Guerrilla warfare, however, knows deep in its heart that something in the world is interesting — and it was on that conviction that Yokoi had built his career. To say "the world is interesting" is common knowledge, of course. But Yokoi, as a guerrilla fighter, had clearly made up his mind: winning isn't everything. So then, wasn't his departure from the company his final act of positioning — his "last stand"?

That the product he poured his heart into as he left Nintendo was the Virtual Boy goes without saying.

p.174 A pitch-dark world gives birth to a world of infinite depth.

3D, as a concept for information devices, goes without saying — but as entertainment, for games and films, it's tremendous. As for stereoscopic television, I'm not sure how that would work, but it should be fantastic. The idea for the Virtual Boy came about before a certain company began selling LED displays — displays that show images in complete darkness. At the same time, a tool used by aircraft mechanics lets you look at diagrams with one eye while simultaneously viewing the actual object with the other. Being able to see a diagram clearly overlaid in the darkness was, for me, quite something.

At that point, to be honest, I wasn't particularly interested in making it into a product. But then, after a while, the Virtual Boy project got started. When it comes to 3D imagery, using liquid crystal is the standard approach — it's the easiest to build, after all. However, liquid p.175 crystal uses a backlight, so even when the screen is made completely dark, a few percent of the light still bleeds through, giving you a grey image. If you draw on liquid crystal and make it pitch dark, you get a faint grey glow on the screen. So even if the basic image is visible, it can't truly convey infinite depth — the human eye just won't perceive it that way. That's why we used LED. With liquid crystal you get grey, but with a true pitch-black background, people naturally sense infinite depth.

There's another point as well. When you draw polygons with liquid crystal and there's slight jagginess, the slightly different angles of the lines in the left and right images make your eyes tire very quickly. It becomes hard to perceive depth. With LED, on the other hand, lines are drawn smoothly, so the eyes find it far easier to achieve stereoscopic vision. And since LEDs draw in dots that are round, you don't feel the border of the screen. This is where LED was different from conventional liquid crystal — it was truly in the dark. So software developers also started saying, "We can draw without a screen border — we can draw only the lines we need — the screen feels incredibly wide." That's what they told us.

As is well known, Virtual Boy ended in failure as a product. The market, having been taken over by enthusiasts, was simply too manic. Yokoi's vision, however, was different from theirs — it was a challenge to bring the world of gaming back to square one, away from a product aimed purely at enthusiasts, toward something for first-time players who weren't yet familiar with games. Virtual Boy was not aimed at enthusiasts; it targeted people who found games a way to kill time. As the saying goes, games today have become extraordinarily complex, demanding that players master highly advanced techniques. Games certainly receive high praise from children and game enthusiasts — but the truth of the matter is that everyone else simply doesn't engage with them at all.

バーチャルボーイのパッケージ — Virtual Boy packaging

p.179 Take arcades as an example: at the height of their popularity, fighting games were the mainstream, and it was mostly children who frequented them. But by the time Virtual Boy came along, young people under twenty had almost entirely stopped going to arcades, and among those in their twenties, a pronounced sense had emerged that "arcades are for graduates" — meaning something one has left behind. Considered across the game industry as a whole, this represented yet another major crisis — the game population was unmistakably shrinking. And this, too, was what Virtual Boy's challenge was about.

So when Virtual Boy proceeded to receive a drubbing of critical evaluation from the game press, even the arcades — which had staked out their own niche — showed it little mercy. The people who evaluate games in those pages tend to say things like, "What a shame — truly a shame," while those who actually play games today are people who have grown accustomed to playing ordinary games; people who are not yet accustomed to them have a tendency to never set foot in a game shop at all. The game industry itself has a tendency to get wrapped up in this. Yokoi's message — "Once more, back to square one with games" — is surely getting through. It is, in fact, getting through.

p.180 Aimed at the people at the bottom of the game market

When moving from the Famicom to the Super Famicom, the message was, "Games this difficult are no good anymore." New games came out in large numbers, so sales figures looked fine on the surface — but the amount of money that game-playing people were willing to invest was shrinking, and so the game population itself was in decline.

The same thing is happening with the Nintendo 64. As long as Nintendo keeps chasing television games — the Famicom, the Super Famicom, the television screen — I wonder if there's really no other way. Once more, wouldn't it be good to pull casual players back in? And to do that, wouldn't you need to make something three-dimensional? A television screen means that, no matter what you put on it, people are going to get bored sooner or later. So for games aimed at the casual end of the market, you need to produce something that, even if it bores the hardcore player, will still draw in the ordinary person. The trouble is that among the hardcore crowd there are people with opinions like, "Games like that are ancient history," and those people simply close their minds to it. Personally speaking, I think we should be targeting people like grandmas and grandpas, uncles and aunties, and children — that's the game we need. People who think that way tend to wash their hands of it, saying, "Oh, he's just that kind of person."

p.181 But the media goes looking for opinions from exactly that kind of enthusiast, so when they say things like "the world has rejected it," that's why people stopped buying — that's what makes it interesting, and yet it isn't, is it?

The Product Liability Law that worked against new things

There's one more thing: around the time of its launch, the Product Liability Law [製造物責任法] came into effect, and I found myself obliged to include a sentence I hadn't planned to write. With TV games it's the same — once you write something like "harmful to the eyes," that phrase gets emphasized, and it ends up becoming a new kind of thing like Virtual Boy, something you just have to say: "Buy it." People who buy it think, "Oh, so it's a bad toy." With the PL Law, there's no getting around it, so manuals end up saying things like "Read the instructions" or "Bad for your body" — that kind of language. Virtual Boy was something genuinely new, and its image suffered for that.

So it was frightening how much it affected people's eyes, and from the start even American scientists said it was, if anything, good for the eyes rather than bad. When we added Virtual Boy to the team to verify its effect on the eyes, the results came back positive — and even as we were appealing that, it vanished from shelves almost overnight.

p.182 It's a shame, really. From the start, we were working with something so light and small — like sunglasses — and the thing is, because it has a CPU inside, it generates noise and becomes a source of interference. But if you could make something no bigger than a pair of sunglasses that didn't produce that interference, then regardless of any radio wave regulations, it would eventually become technically feasible — I don't think that's out of reach.

Whether it hits or misses is fifty-fifty

Virtual Boy was enormously popular at the time of its announcement. But then, when it went on sale, enthusiasts started putting out all kinds of negative opinions. Even the company president was saying things like "just what the enthusiasts say" — and yet they still wanted us to run commercials targeting the general public. When you're trying to create something new, "new" on its own isn't enough. The idea has to be a hit — whether it hits or misses is fifty-fifty. I think something epoch-making is epoch-making precisely because it doesn't always land. But still, "it didn't work out" is no good either, is it?

p.183 That's the only future Nintendo has" — that's the feeling I was going on. And that, from my perspective, was a mistake in the way forward, and we ended up failing. But I do have confidence that Virtual Boy can be put back on track. Within the company, people would often say, "If you give us a hundred million yen in PR budget, we can get it going." That sort of thing was said quite a lot.

Game Boy Pocket

Yokoi intended to leave Nintendo after Virtual Boy. However, Virtual Boy ended up being a commercial failure, and he found himself having no choice but to postpone his plans. This is where Yokoi's complicated feelings toward Nintendo become apparent. He had planned to leave the company on his own terms, at a time of his choosing, and carry out his life's plans at a comfortable pace. He wanted an environment where he could work freely, guided by his own judgment. But, as he says, leaving the company is something that is not so easy to do straightforwardly. He had already passed the age of fifty, and the story of his life up until the point of leaving was not something the world would look upon with simple honesty, he felt.

Originally, Yokoi had wanted to be able to look back on things with a clear conscience. However, because Nintendo had grown dissatisfied with him for leaving — or so it was widely assumed — and because he bore responsibility for Virtual Boy, it can easily be imagined that he was pointed at by others even as he resigned.

p.185 That is precisely why Yokoi loved Nintendo as a company, and why Nintendo took him in. "I truly feel grateful to Nintendo for raising me to where I am today," he says. "So I can't simply leave without feeling that debt of gratitude. That's why I extended my time before retiring, and as a final gift — a parting souvenir, if you will — I pushed forward with the development of the Game Boy Pocket."

The Game Boy Pocket took the Game Boy, a device designed exclusively for gaming, and made it even more compact. This pocket was able to fit inside the pockets of clothing and the like — making it small enough to carry around — and in that sense it felt like the culmination of the entire Game Boy series.

From the Handy to the Pocket

I myself had intended to leave Nintendo after Virtual Boy, and when I did resign as planned, this failure p.186 weighed on me and I felt I had to take responsibility. The world would see it that way too. So, having been given a certain amount of leeway, I developed the Game Boy Pocket as a kind of parting gift before leaving Nintendo — something I could offer up as a final product.

The original Game Boy already had plenty of room to spare, you see — parts added for electromagnetic interference shielding, and so on, making the size quite large. In practice, though, none of that turned out to be strictly necessary. For example, manganese batteries had been the assumed power source at first, but alkaline batteries became the mainstream. And since AA batteries were now being used, the battery capacity doubled, meaning you could get the same battery life in something even smaller. So couldn't we make it smaller? That was the idea. Battery life with AA batteries is ten hours — that was our target.

Now, the original Game Boy, for all sorts of reasons at the time, ended up growing quite large. So it could be said that the Handy size became bigger than it needed to be. That's why, even though calling it pocket-sized might be a stretch, there was a sense that it could fit in a pocket.

p.187 And you know, a friend of mine bought a mobile phone. When I asked why, he said, "It used to be too big and I didn't like it, but now it's gotten this small." So if you make it small enough to fit in your pocket, new users will start to pick it up, I thought. So would it fit in an inner pocket? We actually checked the size, and after three or four days the results came back — it would fit in a breast pocket. That's how the pocket-sized dimensions came about.

The Game Boy Is "the Basics of Gaming"

The Game Boy, from the very beginning, rode what you might call a "quiet boom." It always sold at a steady pace. Software houses, though, went with Super Famicom software — because the profit margins were naturally higher given the high prices. So they made software for that, and the software houses built up a solid reputation for it. But on our side, we steadily developed software, working earnestly away at it, and the Game Boy itself has earned strong reviews. The Game Boy, I believe, advances in the direction of three-dimensionality in games — but the Game Boy is "the basics of gaming," you see. Games like Tetris — publishing games with television-style gameplay that keep releasing new colors and features in endless p.188 variations — I think they hold more than enough potential to boom all over again.
