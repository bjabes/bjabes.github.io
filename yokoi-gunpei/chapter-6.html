The Philosophy of Gunpei Yokoi

1997–20XX

[IMAGE: Ultra Hand illustration]

p.191 Gunpei Yokoi's Upbringing

Between interviews about products, we had the chance to hear about Yokoi's childhood and student years. In a word, his youth was like something straight out of a movie. His special talents were piano and ballroom dancing; his hobbies, cars and skin diving. He was so popular with girls that it was almost a problem. You could practically call him the "Young Master of Kyoto" — that's how charmed his youth was. And yet, even within this movie-star life, he had never once forgotten his love of tinkering and making things, going all the way back to childhood. Yokoi himself said he couldn't quite explain why he was so passionately devoted to making things. The household he grew up in was a perfectly ordinary salaryman family. There was nothing in particular about the home atmosphere that was oriented toward engineering or the natural sciences, he said. If one were to search for any episode along those lines, there is a story about Yokoi's grandfather. His grandfather was extraordinarily skilled with his hands — he wrote out the entire text of "Kimigayo" with a brush on a single grain of rice and presented it to the Emperor. Yokoi p.192 himself apparently believes that his dexterity was something inherited from his grandfather.

The Encounter with Piano Was My Starting Point

I'm the youngest of four siblings. As a child, I never particularly thought of myself as dexterous or anything like that.

When I was a kid, my father bought a piano. I was made to take lessons against my will. Every Saturday the teacher would come, and all four of us siblings were taught, but starting from the oldest, they dropped out one by one, and I was the only one left.

The piano forged a strong connection for me between music, mathematics, and the natural sciences. So music has been enormously important in my life. My life of making things may have started there. My grades as a child weren't very good, but science and music were the only subjects I did well in.

p.193 Obsessed with Model Trains — My First Appearance in the Media

When I was in elementary school, I begged my father to buy me an O-gauge model train set, and I was instantly hooked. When I got to middle school, a magazine came to do a story on it, and that was my very first appearance in the media (laughs). My father was an extreme neat freak, so if I set up a fixed layout like a diorama for the model trains, he'd get angry. So I had to make the layout foldable and things like that. Working within that kind of constraint is probably what feeds into the way I make things today. When I was a kid, all there was were tin toys and candle-powered pop-pop boats, so you had no choice but to make your own fun.

Anyway, I was absolutely obsessed. I was kneading paper clay for the diorama, and I kept at it without taking a single break, until I just collapsed. I found out later that my shoulders had gotten so stiff it caused a dizzy spell (laughs). That's how absorbed I was.

p.194 Joined the Judo Club, Fought a Kodokan Second-Dan. Quit After Breaking My Collarbone

In high school, I briefly joined the judo club. I'd been commuting to school by bicycle since I was a kid, so my legs and hips were strong, and on top of that, by high school I'd gotten pretty heavy — around 80 kilos. So the judo club recruited me. When I joined, I immediately threw a black belt clean off his feet. Because of that, they put me in an inter-school match. But then my opponent turned out to be a Kodokan second-dan. A Kodokan second-dan is on a completely different level from an ordinary second-dan black belt. He threw me full force, and I broke my collarbone (laughs). So I quit the judo club. My entire judo club career lasted just two weeks.

When I Took Up Ballroom Dancing, the Girls Came Flocking

After my collarbone healed, it just so happened that there was a ballroom dancing instructor living right behind my house, so I took up dancing. When I was in university, I entered a competition as an individual and placed second in the tango p.195 and so on. The reason I started dancing was, of course, because I thought it would make me popular with girls (laughs). And indeed it did. But I became too popular, and that was a problem (laughs).

With dancing, you pair up with a female partner, right? And you practice with her every day in training clothes. Before you know it, you can't do the dance without her, and it starts to feel like you're a married couple. Naturally, your partner starts wanting to get married. But that wasn't my intention at all. And it wasn't just one partner, so — yeah, it was a real problem. The reason I quit dancing was exactly that. I quit because I was too popular (laughs).

The thing about dance is that you really have to think in terms of human ergonomics. So even now when I play golf, I can master the form right away — I can easily imitate the movements of a skilled player. That's another expression of my dexterity.

Making Things Was Still the Center of My Life

When I say all this, it might sound like I had a glamorous youth — like something out of "The Young Master of Kyoto" — but p.196 making things was always my main focus. When I started working on something, even if girls came to ask me out on dates, I turned them all down. I'd silently work on my projects late into the night. But the funny thing about girls is this: when you turn down a date, it only makes them want to ask you out more (laughs). And so I ended up being even more popular (laughs).

Making things was always at the center of my life, so I was truly happy when I joined Nintendo. Until then, I'd had to buy materials with my own pocket money, but now the company bought them for me — and on top of that, they even paid me a salary. I just couldn't get enough of it. When long holidays came around, the stress would actually build up. I'd be thinking, "I can't wait to get back to work."

Driving Around and Developing the World's First Car Stereo

I loved cars too, and I drove around quite a lot. Naturally, I tried installing a car stereo — probably the world's first. Back then, when it came to cars, all you had was a radio, so I connected a tape recorder to the radio. But of course, p.197 while driving, the vibrations would distort the sound. So I came up with my own mechanism and modified it, and so on.

It was three or four years later, you know, that Clarion released their car stereo. So my friends and I would laugh and say, "What took them so long? We've been doing that for ages."

I'd record baseball game broadcasts, and then when I was driving around late at night and a car pulled up next to me at a red light, I'd play back the broadcast at full volume. The guy next to me would frantically start turning his radio dial, searching for the channel that was broadcasting the baseball game. That was so funny, you know. Because back then, nobody imagined you could make recordings like that.

As for club activities in college, I wasn't really all that enthusiastic. I had a beautiful boy-soprano voice (laughs), so I was in a chorus group, but in the end I had more fun hanging out with friends, and I got really into skin diving. I'd make my own speargun, and use it to catch fish. It's about a hundred kilometers from Kyoto to the Sea of Japan, right? I'd drive out there every week.

p.198 My Love of Making Things Comes from Being a Natural-Born Ringleader

The reason I became so absorbed in making things is probably because I had something of a natural-born ringleader temperament. That feeling of showing people what you've made and boasting, "How about that?" Among people who love making things, there are those who enjoy the act of creating for themselves, and those who enjoy showing what they've made to others and surprising them — I'm completely the type who wants to surprise people. When I think about it, both now and when I was a child, the greatest thrill has always been seeing other people's delight at something I've made. In that sense, I've really been doing the same thing my whole life, ever since I was a kid.

When making something, I'm not the type to just build and fix, build and fix as I go. I think everything through in my head to the very end, and only once the whole thing has solidified do I move into production. I learned from experience that if you start on a wing and a prayer, you inevitably end up having to redo things. So even after I joined Nintendo, the first thing I'd do was visualize the final finished product. Then I'd think through each individual part. When you work that way, what you've planned reliably takes shape. That's why my department had fewer projects fall through than others at Nintendo. I've received feedback from my subordinates, too, that my instructions are clear and the direction we're heading is always well-defined.

p.199 There really are almost no products of mine that ended up scrapped. Of course, I'm scrapping them in my head before I ever start building. With manufacturing, if you don't proceed with a thoroughly prepared plan, things often end up being a waste. So when I say "I could pull off the perfect crime," it would absolutely be a perfect crime. (Laughs)

p.200 For Those Who Aspire to Be Creators

Among the readers of this book, there are surely many who aspire to work in product planning and development just like Yokoi, or who hope to become game creators. In today's world, where virtually every industry has fully matured, enormous barriers stand in the way of anyone trying to make their entrance into the world. The sheer volume of knowledge one must acquire has swelled to staggering proportions. "Don't become a narrow specialist. Be a generalist" — you hear this kind of advice everywhere, but there is perhaps nothing that better exemplifies the saying "easier said than done." Without a certain degree of specialized knowledge, you can't accomplish even small tasks, let alone take on major projects. Yet if you spend all your time absorbing specialized knowledge, you become exactly that — a narrow specialist. Striking the right balance here is extremely difficult.

How should one approach this dilemma, and how can it be overcome? We asked Yokoi for advice that readers of this book p.201 could take to heart — words of advice addressed to them.

For Engineers, Abandoning Pride Is Essential

First of all, engineers who are specialists tend to have this misconception that you can't make anything unless you use difficult technology. But if I were to put myself in the user's position and think about making something, I'd start by considering what can be made most quickly and easily.

Grand, elaborate machines come about when simple things gradually develop and evolve over time. But engineers try to jump straight to building that final, elaborate machine with all their might, and that's how wasteful things get made. Really, it's perfectly fine to start with something like a simple tool, and then if you flesh it out from there, you might end up with something in between — or surprisingly, the thing you made at the very beginning might turn out to have been the best one all along. Those are the kinds of things that lead to hit products. So my development philosophy is this: commit yourself fully to selling, and abandon the engineer's pride. Everything I've done up to now, guided by that belief, has been a reasonable hit, so p.202 I don't think that's too far off the mark as a way of thinking.

So, you know, even with a machine for chopping onions — using a motor to chop might be fine, but if you think from the user's perspective and decide that shaving it with a plane is faster, then that's the real product, isn't it?

Don't try to do everything yourself. Think about leaving things to the specialists.

I don't have any specialized technical expertise of my own. I only have a vague, general awareness of things. When it comes time to make something and you need this technology or that technology, rather than studying it all yourself before getting started, the right thing to do is to gather specialists. But when you do that, the key person in development gets scared of being told, "So what exactly have you been doing?" — and ends up studying everything on their own. I'm saying that's what leads to mistakes.

If you gather specialists and have them build it, of course you're going to end up with the best possible product. That's what leads to hit products.

Also, there are very few people in the world who can see the big picture like that. Everyone studies technology in narrow, deep p.203 specialties, so they end up adopting the attitude of acquiring expertise in that one area alone. Well, if they don't do that, they won't have anything to distinguish themselves as engineers. So from an engineer's perspective, someone like me would be dismissed as "What is this dropout?" But once you've produced two hit products, the word "dropout" disappears somewhere along the way.

Don't become an engineer. Become a producer.

This idea of wanting to produce things — if you say it's not very engineer-like, well, that's true. Engineers are always afraid of being told, "Well then, you try doing it yourself."

My way of working is to delegate, saying, "I don't understand anything beyond this point, so I'm counting on you." And then I say, "Leave the connections between things to me" — that's how we divide up responsibilities. When you do that, each group does their best to figure things out and contributes their highest wisdom. But if I were to give high-handed orders saying, "Do it this way," the group won't budge. They'll say, "If you're going to go that far, we'll just do what you say," and they won't offer their own ideas p.204 at all. But if I leave things to them, the engineers get motivated and come up with all sorts of proposals. Of course, those proposals end up involving other groups as well, so I'm the one who coordinates everything.

What a producer needs is not specialist knowledge, but a fundamental understanding of how things work

Of course, a producer needs to have a broad, shallow knowledge of technology, but really, elementary school science — natural science at that level — is sufficient. Take semiconductors, for example. You don't need to understand what's inside them. There are specialists whose job it is to think about the internals, so it's absurd to try to tell them what to do about that. Just leave it to them.

What we call technical ability isn't actually about studying. It's about understanding the fundamental logic of how things work. If you understand the underlying logic, you can keep finding new applications one after another. Being able to do difficult calculations isn't what makes someone impressive — what matters is knowing what those calculations are good for. It's not about knowing the fine details.

p.205 Young people should have the perspective of an outsider and the courage to act on it

A systematic, established industry certainly has a sense of stagnation about it, but I think part of that comes from only looking at things from within that industry. When someone in a free position like mine comes along and connects things with a completely different idea from the outside, people are genuinely surprised. To break through that sense of stagnation, someone needs to take a fresh look from the outside. If you're going to sit there agonizing within your own group, why not go take in the scenery outside for a change?

People inevitably get bound by established concepts and precedent, so I want young people to have the courage to break through that. That said, I was probably fortunate to have joined an immature company like Nintendo. There was no development department or anything like that. That's precisely why I was able to commit fully to the principle that "what sells is what's best for the company." Nobody objected, nobody criticized me, and nobody ever said things like "your technical skills are this or that."

p.206 The real problem is with those who stand above young people

In today's world, if a young person tries to do something bold and new, it's obvious they'll be suppressed from above. So conversely, I'd like to urge those in positions of authority to stop suppressing them. Specifically, when a young person comes up with an idea, don't look at it through the lens of "this is just a young person" — what's important is to consider it seriously.

During my time at Nintendo, I put a great deal of thought into how to get new employees to speak up in meetings. Because once a young person starts thinking "someone like me has no business speaking up," it's all over. I tried things like holding meetings with a banquet-like atmosphere, or deliberately saying ridiculous things myself to break the ice — I came up with all sorts of approaches. During my own days as a new employee, there was nobody around to oppose what a young upstart like me had to say, so those in positions above need to create a similar environment.

For example, suppose I and one young employee both proposed the same thing — the world would absolutely trust what I say. And that's the problem. When a young person speaks up about something, p.207 The willingness to find a way to make use of what they say — that's what truly matters. That is the ability of those who stand above others. And if even one product turns out well as a result, that young person gains tremendous confidence. They stop saying careless or half-hearted things. They start studying furiously behind the scenes before speaking up. And if a hit product comes out of it, naturally the question arises: "Who came up with that product?" At that point, the person above inevitably starts saying "It was me, it was me," and ends up stealing the credit from their subordinate. And then the person above *that* person might be thinking the same thing. (Laughs) When you listen to various business leaders talk, it's fascinating how different they all are. Some say, "Everything my employees did was really done by me," while others say, "That was done by my employees — I'm just a manager." I think the person who says "I'm just a manager" is the more impressive one. I think that kind of person can accomplish great things.

As for discussing what young people should do — that turns into a conversation about changing the entire country. The quickest solution is for those above to change. Rather than telling young people what to do, what I really want to say is this: I want the people in positions of authority to show greater generosity of spirit.

p.208 How to Make "Products That Sell"

The Mistaken Thinking That Engineers Easily Fall Into

Over the thirty years I spent at a company called Nintendo making all kinds of products, I was shown time and again the ways engineers think and the tendencies that lead them into mistaken ideas. How was it that I myself, an engineer, managed — however imperfectly — to get products out into the world? Where exactly does the average engineer's way of thinking go wrong? I'd like to touch on that point.

What I've been working in is the world of entertainment products. Entertainment products are an entirely different world from practical goods. Practical goods are things that make life more convenient, or that make tasks faster, p.209 or kitchen goods — they're all practical. In contrast, entertainment products are "unnecessary, non-urgent goods." In other words, not needed, not urgent. Put another way, they're goods that people can do without. This is the kind of thing I've been involved in — the world of entertainment.

Practical products have something called needs. You identify needs and develop products to meet them — that's the natural approach. But what are the needs for unnecessary, non-urgent products? Put simply, it's "killing time." But uncovering the needs around killing time is not so easy. "What would be fun to do?" — that's something quite hard to pin down. This was the backdrop against which I thought about things. Wanting to peek behind a locker, or having nothing to do on the bullet train — those kinds of small things are actually important as needs for product development.

What Users "Don't Ask For"

These needs — who is expressing them? The user, of course. The people who use things, the people who play. And the person who listens to those needs and builds things — that's the engineer. Originally, product development is something where needs p.210 are heard by engineers who then commercialize them — that's the normal pattern. However, I believe there is a very significant pitfall here. The thing is, engineers have something called pride. Most engineers have it. When a certain user voices a certain need, and the engineer hears it and sets about building the product, the engineer starts adding things with their own technology — "We can do this too, we can do that too" — going beyond what the user is actually asking for. That's why with things like microwave ovens, there are so many that people can't figure out how to use. Those extra features are just engineers playing around. "Adding this only costs 10 yen more," "Adding that only costs 20 yen more" — but when all those small additions pile up, the total becomes an outrageous sum, and on top of that, people can't figure out how to use the thing. That's the worst possible outcome.

When I was developing products, conveying to engineers what the user wants was simple enough. However, figuring out what the user does not want — that is extremely difficult. For example, if it comes down to choosing between monochrome and color, anyone would obviously say color is better. But is that really what the user is asking for? Very few people can explain, "The downside is that the batteries drain faster and the product price goes up."

p.211 Being able to make that judgment on your own and explaining to the engineers, "The user is saying this, but the real need is actually this" — having a person who serves as that interface is absolutely essential.

"Amazing Products" Are Not What Users Need

Last year, in August 1996, I left Nintendo and founded a company called Koto. When I did, I was struck by how few people there are doing this kind of work. All sorts of organizations, from major companies down to small ones, came to me to learn about how to plan products and how to generate ideas. What I tell them is this: "Create a person who serves as the interface. That person does not need to be a brilliant engineer. Put someone with good sense, someone with refined sensibilities, in that role. If you have engineers doing it directly, they'll say 'we can do this, we can do that,' and end up creating an amazing product. But amazing products are not what users need. Products that sell are what's needed. Give the interface job to someone who can take responsibility for saying, 'What should we leave out?' — have that kind of person handle p.212 it, and have that person be the center of your product planning." That's what it comes down to.

This interface work is actually not all that difficult — if you just pay attention to one thing, it's simple. I've experienced this myself: when you listen to what users say and start building something yourself, somehow this desire to show off creeps in. Even when drawing up blueprints, you think, "If I just add one more line here, it'll be so much more convenient." But later you realize just how wrong that one line was. To put it even more extremely: you've drawn a beautifully clean blueprint. Then later you realize one line is unnecessary. But erasing it would mess up the drawing, so you think, "I'll just submit it as is." Engineers will go that far. If you have someone who isn't directly involved in the product — someone free to speak their mind — controlling the process, you can eliminate this kind of waste and create more efficient products, don't you think?

Don't Fall in Love with Technology — Use Lateral Thinking

And then there's another thing: you can't help but fall in love with your own plans.

p.213 During the Nintendo era too, people like that would come in trying to sell their ideas. You'd listen to what they had to say, and they'd be frothing at the mouth explaining it, completely enamored with their own idea — but if you listened from the sidelines, it was obviously a worthless idea. Engineers are prone to falling into that kind of trap. What I always ask myself is this: suppose a product I planned is sitting on the shelf at a department store. Then, pretending it's not something I came up with, would I actually pay money to buy it? I'm constantly asking myself that question. I believe it's extremely important to always review the work you're doing through the eyes of a third party.

On top of that, engineers want to show off their technical skills, so they dream of using incredible cutting-edge technology. That becomes a major mistake in product development. You end up with products that don't sell, products that are too expensive. Where does cutting-edge technology come from? Very often it's military technology. Microwave ovens, for example — those were developed as weapons before making their way into the home. When engineers come to understand a technology, they can't help but want to use it somehow. But there's no way something like that will be priced right for ordinary consumer goods. It just ends up being too expensive to sell.

What I always say is: "Wait for that technology to mature." In other words, the tech- p.214 nology becomes widespread, and prices keep dropping. That's the sweet spot to aim for. For example, if we had tried to release the Game & Watch five years earlier, it would have been a machine costing 100,000 yen. That's how much calculators cost back then. But mass production effects brought the price down and down until it reached 3,800 yen. And that's why it became a hit. This is what I call "Lateral Thinking with Seasoned Technology." In other words, you take mature technology and think about it laterally. If you think about it vertically, it stays as a calculator — just a calculator and nothing more. But if you think about it laterally, what else can be done with it? If you consider alternative uses like that, all sorts of ideas start emerging, don't they?

Let your subordinates take the credit, and the fruits will come back to you

Finally, I'd like to talk about how to cultivate that kind of developer. The people who develop products are engineers, but engineers have an enormous amount of pride, which makes them difficult to manage. During my time at Nintendo, I had sixty engineers under me, and what I found was the most important thing for getting them to perform at one hundred percent was to put them in the spotlight.

p.215 What I mean is this: a young engineer comes up with a wonderful idea and proposes it to his boss. Then, in many cases, the boss claims that achievement as his own and takes it one level higher. When that happens, the young engineer will never come up with another idea again. It just feels pointless. Because I recognized this, whenever an engineer came up with an idea, I would bring him along to the president's office and say, "This is what he proposed." You need at least that much generosity.

On top of that, what I did was this: when I came up with an idea myself and filed a patent application, I would file it under the name of the subordinate who had helped develop it. So if you look through Nintendo's records, about half of the inventions that were actually mine don't have my name on them. I told all of those subordinates, "I'm putting it under your name." By doing that, eventually they would work their hardest for me. When you manage young people with that kind of attitude, they become tremendously motivated.

p.216 The hollowing out of industry is simply a shortage of ideas

When I think about making something, my philosophy is to create something that is the only one of its kind in the world — something being made for the first time ever. The reason is simple: there is no competition, no rivalry. Japanese companies are expanding overseas at a rapid pace. They do so because they can't compete unless they manufacture cheaply with inexpensive labor. But the way I see it, that's not the issue. The idea that you can't sell something unless you make it cheaply — that's simply a shortage of ideas. So my view is: why not come up with ideas good enough that products made right here in Japan can still sell at a high price? And achieving that doesn't require doing anything particularly difficult — it can be done through the most simple and unassuming means.

When I look at Sony's "Walkman," I think it's truly remarkable. Was the Walkman something that could only have been made with Sony's technical prowess? Absolutely not. Any other company could have easily built one, if only they had seen the Walkman first. And yet the idea for the Walkman was something only Sony came up with.

Products that sell can be created if you approach the task earnestly. If you use cutting-edge technology, you'll end up making products that actually don't sell. So if you approach things with a relaxed mindset, using "Lateral Thinking with Seasoned Technology," there are still plenty of products out there waiting to be made.

p.218 Gunpei Yokoi: What Comes Next

This interview was conducted in 1997.

— You left Nintendo and founded a company called Koto. What specifically will you be doing going forward?

I worked at Nintendo for thirty years, so I believe I'm well versed in the world of play. Because of that, I'd like to move Koto's work forward with the world of play as its core focus. I'm also thinking about game hardware that could replace television games — things that have never existed before. That's still a trade secret, though, so I can't go into detail until about three years from now. (Laughs.)

I also don't want to limit myself solely to the world of play — I'd like to try my hand at the world of practical products as well. People from the practical products world are often surprised when they come to hear what I have to say, so I have a feeling that I could make it in that world too. In the world of practical products, p.219 when you look at things from the perspective of play, you start to see a different direction. So while entertainment is the main focus, I don't want to limit myself to that — I'd like to challenge myself in all sorts of fields.

— Can the worlds of practical products and play really be connected that easily?

Even among practical products, there are quite a few these days that incorporate humorous expression, and I think the ideas for that are limitless. I believe that people from the practical products world and someone like me from the world of play can complement each other's shortcomings and create something new together.

To begin with, I have absolutely no interest in breaking into mature markets. My goal is to combine play with something else and create entirely new markets.

— Is there a specific practical product market you have your sights set on?

For example, I think the medical field would be really interesting. When we were working on the Virtual Boy, the PL Act p.220 the Product Liability Act came up, and I had the chance to speak with people in the medical field. When I did, the idea came up that incorporating game elements into areas like rehabilitation would be extremely beneficial. That's exactly the kind of connection between my area of expertise and the practical field of medicine. Rehabilitation involves repeating the same thing every day, so nobody really wants to do it. But by incorporating game elements into it, rehabilitation can progress tremendously. They end up doing it even when you tell them to stop. Imagine children getting so absorbed that their rehabilitation progresses rapidly, until the doctor watching can finally say, "Okay, that's enough for today" — that would be wonderful.

This kind of work is hard to do in a large organization like Nintendo. But for a small company like Koto, you can be nimble and handle things with fine attention to detail.

— So the scope of your work will expand beyond the world of games as well, then.

Up until now, in the world of games, I've been practicing "Lateral Thinking with Seasoned Technology" — in other words, rather than applying a given technology straightforwardly as-is, finding ways to put it to use through a completely different way of thinking. I've been doing p.221 that up until now. But the world of games has matured to a certain degree and is reaching a dead end. That said, it's not as though "fun," which is the essence of games, has disappeared. What I want to think about is what would happen if we connected the fun of games to fields like medicine or practical products. You might call it 'Lateral Thinking with Seasoned Games,' I suppose. (Laughs.)
